Planning a move to Panama is exciting, but there's one part of the process many people overlook: preparing, ahead of time, the U.S. documents they may need after leaving the country.

Depending on your situation, you may need background checks, birth or marriage certificates, corporate documents, notarized documents, apostilles, certified translations, or other official records.

Organizing these documents while you're still in the United States can make your transition much smoother. And if you're already living abroad, having someone in the U.S. who can coordinate the process on your behalf can save you time, travel, and complications.

## Why U.S. documents can matter when moving to Panama

Many documents intended for international use must be issued by the appropriate authority in the United States and, depending on the type of document and how it will be used, may require additional authentication or an apostille.

For someone preparing a move to Panama, this can include documents needed for:

- Immigration or residency processes
- Family-related procedures
- Business activities
- Banking processes
- Legal procedures
- Other administrative matters

Requirements can vary depending on the type of document, the state where it was issued, and the purpose for which it will be used.

For example, if you're applying for the [Friendly Nations Visa](/en/blog/friendly-nations-visa-guide/) or the [Pensionado Visa](/en/blog/pensionado-visa-guide/), immigration authorities commonly ask for an apostilled background check. And if you're opening an account with a [Panamanian bank](/en/blog/banking-in-panama-guide/), certified corporate or financial documents are often required.

That's why it's important to know the specific requirements before requesting, shipping, or apostilling a document.

## U.S. documents you might need

Some of the documents most frequently requested from people moving to or living abroad include:

- FBI background checks
- State-level background checks
- Birth certificates
- Marriage certificates
- Divorce decrees or records
- Death certificates
- Corporate documents
- Certificates of Good Standing
- Notarized financial or supporting documents
- Certified translations

Not everyone will need all of these documents. The specific requirements will depend on your personal situation and the purpose for which the documents will be used.

## What is an apostille?

An apostille is a form of authentication used to certify the origin of a public document when it will be used in another country that participates in the Hague Apostille Convention.

In simple terms, an apostille helps verify the authenticity of the document, or of the signature and seal on it, so that it can be used internationally.

However, getting an apostille doesn't always mean simply sending a document to an apostille office. The correct procedure can depend on whether the document is state- or federal-level, how it was issued, and whether it needs additional steps before it can be apostilled.

For this reason, document preparation can become complicated — especially when the person is already outside the United States.

## What happens if you're already outside the United States?

One of the main challenges for Americans living abroad is figuring out how to obtain U.S. documents without having to return to the country in person.

In some cases, documents may need to be requested from a specific state or federal agency, notarized, authenticated, translated, apostilled, and then shipped abroad.

Coordinating several documents at the same time can also be complicated when different agencies, states, or federal offices are involved.

Working with a U.S.-based document service can make it easier to coordinate the process and allow much of it to be handled remotely.
