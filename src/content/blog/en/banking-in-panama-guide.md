Panama is one of Latin America's most important banking centers. It is also one of the harder countries in the region for a foreign newcomer to open a personal bank account. Those two facts coexist, and understanding both will save you a lot of frustration when you arrive.

This guide covers what banks actually require, which institutions are more accessible to foreigners, what the timeline looks like, and how to manage your finances while the account is pending.

## Why Panama banking is stricter than you expect

Panama went through a difficult period internationally. In 2019, the country was placed on the FATF (Financial Action Task Force) grey list — a designation for countries with deficiencies in anti-money laundering and counter-terrorism financing controls. The grey listing had real consequences: correspondent banking relationships became strained, international transactions faced additional scrutiny, and local banks responded by tightening their compliance procedures significantly.

Panama was removed from the FATF grey list in 2023 after implementing substantial regulatory reforms. But the internal bank compliance culture that developed during that period has not reversed. Banks are still very cautious with new foreign customers, particularly those without an established financial history in Panama.

The result is that opening an account as a recently arrived foreigner — even with legitimate income, good credit, and straightforward finances — requires more documentation than most expats expect, and some banks will decline the application outright.

## What banks require

Requirements vary by institution, but the following documents are consistently requested across most Panamanian banks:

- **Valid passport** (original, not a copy)
- **Secondary ID** (driver's license or national ID from your home country)
- **Proof of Panamanian address** — even a utility bill in your name from a rental, or a letter from your landlord, usually works
- **Proof of income** — typically the last 3 months of bank statements from your home country account, showing regular deposits
- **Source of funds letter** — a written explanation of where your money comes from (employment income, pension, rental income, business revenue, etc.)
- **Reference letter from your home bank** — a formal letter on bank letterhead confirming your relationship, account standing, and character as a customer
- **Tax return or declaration** from your home country (some banks request this, others do not)

For applicants whose residency is in process or already approved, immigration documentation (your residency application receipt, provisional ID, or permanent residence card) will also be requested.

### The reference letter requirement

The bank reference letter deserves specific attention because it trips people up. Many home country banks no longer issue these letters as a standard service — they consider it a legacy practice. In the United States, for example, getting a reference letter from a major retail bank like Chase or Bank of America often requires visiting a branch manager in person and explicitly requesting it, and some branches will decline or charge a fee.

If your home bank will not provide a reference letter, alternatives that have worked for others include: a letter from a financial advisor who manages assets for you, a letter from a credit union, or a letter from a local attorney in Panama who can vouch for your professional relationship. Talk to your immigration attorney about what the specific bank you are targeting is currently accepting.

## Which banks are more accessible to foreigners

Not all banks in Panama have the same appetite for foreign customers. Based on consistent reports from the expat and relocation community:

### More accessible

**Multibank** is frequently cited as one of the most foreigner-friendly banks in Panama. They have experience working with international clients and their compliance team is accustomed to evaluating foreign income documentation.

**Banistmo** (affiliated with HSBC's former Panama operations, now part of Bancolombia group) has branches throughout the country and experience with international customers. Their documentation requirements are thorough but their process is generally predictable.

**Banco General** is one of Panama's largest and most reputable banks. They are selective, but they have a dedicated private banking and international clients division that handles foreign applications more systematically than general retail branches.

### More difficult for foreign newcomers

**Banesco** and **Caja de Ahorros** (a state bank primarily serving Panamanian government employees) are generally harder for foreign applicants without an established presence in Panama. This is not a firm rule, but the experiences reported are more mixed.

**Important caveat:** Bank policies change, and the experience at a specific branch depends heavily on the relationship manager you work with. A personal introduction from a Panama-based attorney or a mutual professional contact can significantly change how an application is received.

## Non-resident accounts: essentially impossible

If you are visiting Panama as a tourist — even planning to move there — opening a bank account without any residency status is nearly impossible at most mainstream banks. Panama does not offer the non-resident banking arrangements that some other offshore banking centers provide.

In practice, you generally need one of the following to have a realistic chance:

- A Panama residency visa (permanent or temporary) already approved
- An immigration application that has been formally submitted (with a receipt)
- A business incorporation in Panama with active commercial activity

Some banks will interview prospective customers in process and prepare the account application in advance, to be completed once residency documents are in hand. Ask the bank directly whether they offer this.

## Timeline: from application to account

Once you have all your documents and submit an application at a branch, the timeline to account opening varies:

- Best case (all documents in order, bank comfortable with your profile): **1 to 2 weeks**
- Typical case: **2 to 4 weeks**, including time for the compliance team to review
- More complex cases (higher income from multiple sources, business activity, etc.): **4 to 6 weeks**

Applications are sometimes rejected after the compliance review even when all documents were submitted correctly. If this happens, the bank usually will not tell you the specific reason — it's a compliance decision. The practical response is to try a different institution.

## Managing your finances while waiting

The period between arriving in Panama and having an active local bank account is real, and it can last months. Here is what most people use during that time:

**Wise** (formerly TransferWise) is widely used by expats. It allows you to hold balances in multiple currencies including USD, receive international transfers, and use a debit card for local spending. It is not a Panamanian bank account, but it handles day-to-day expenses well.

**Revolut** is another option, particularly popular with European and British expats. Similar functionality to Wise.

**Cash.** Panama is a cash economy outside of major supermarkets and restaurants in Panama City. ATMs are widely available, and Panama uses USD — no currency conversion needed for Americans. Withdrawal fees at Panamanian ATMs are typically $3 to $5 per transaction.

**Keeping a home-country account active** is important during this period. Many banks in your home country will close dormant accounts after 6 to 12 months of inactivity. Maintaining a small balance and occasional transactions keeps the account open.

## Business banking

If you are starting or operating a business in Panama, the requirements are more extensive than personal accounts. In addition to the standard personal documentation, banks will request:

- Corporate registration documents (certified copy from the Public Registry)
- Corporate bylaws
- Certificate of good standing
- Shareholder and director information (with full KYC documentation for each)
- Business plan or description of operations
- Proof of business activity (contracts, invoices, website, etc.)

Business accounts at most banks require in-person meetings with a relationship manager. Plan for a timeline of 4 to 8 weeks from first meeting to account opening.

## Practical tips

**Come prepared, not just ready.** Don't schedule your bank appointment and then ask what you need. Contact the bank's international or private banking division in advance, ask for the full current checklist, and assemble everything before you walk in. Missing one document typically means rescheduling.

**Get a professional introduction if you can.** A letter of introduction from a Panama-based attorney, accountant, or real estate professional who already works with the bank significantly improves how your application is handled. Banks in Panama still operate significantly on relationships.

**Don't rely solely on one bank.** Apply to two banks simultaneously if possible. If one declines or takes longer, you are not stuck waiting.

**Ask specifically about the compliance timeline.** When you submit documents, ask the branch manager directly: when does your compliance team typically review new customer applications? Getting a specific date-range lets you follow up appropriately without pestering them too frequently.
