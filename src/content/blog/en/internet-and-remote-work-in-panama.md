A client call dropped twice in the same hour from a rental in Bocas del Toro. The host had advertised "high-speed internet." What that meant, in practice, was 8 Mbps shared across four units on a copper line that had been strung along the same poles since the 2000s. The renter switched to a $60/month Starlink kit two days later and never had the problem again. That gap — between what a listing promises and what a connection actually delivers — is the single biggest infrastructure surprise for people planning to work remotely from Panama.

The short version: Panama's internet is genuinely good in the right buildings and genuinely unreliable in the wrong ones, and the difference has almost nothing to do with the neighborhood's reputation.

## Why this matters more than rent or weather

Most relocation guides rank Panama City against Boquete against Coronado on cost of living and climate. For a remote worker, connectivity reliability is a bigger variable than either of those, because a missed client call or a dropped video interview costs more than a few extra dollars a month in rent ever will. Panama's national averages look fine on paper — the country regularly posts fixed broadband speeds above 80 Mbps in international rankings — but that number is an average across dense urban fiber and rural copper, and it tells you almost nothing about any specific address.

## Average speeds by region

These are realistic, sustained speeds reported by remote workers and expat forums in each area, not the "up to" numbers ISPs advertise.

| Area | Typical fiber/cable speed | Reliability | Starlink needed as backup? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Panama City (Costa del Este, San Francisco) | 100-500 Mbps | High | Rarely |
| Panama City (Casco Viejo, older buildings) | 20-80 Mbps | Medium | Sometimes |
| Boquete | 15-50 Mbps | Medium | Often, for video calls |
| Coronado / Playa coastal corridor | 20-60 Mbps | Medium-High | Occasionally |
| Bocas del Toro (Isla Colón) | 5-20 Mbps | Low-Medium | Usually |
| Volcán / remote highlands | 3-10 Mbps | Low | Yes |

The pattern holds across the country: fiber in dense, newer construction is excellent; copper or shared-line connections in older buildings or outlying towns are the weak link, regardless of how upscale the area otherwise feels.

## The real ISP options

### Fiber and cable providers

Three companies cover most of the country: **+Cable** (formerly Cable Onda), **Tigo**, and **Claro**. In Panama City and David, fiber plans from +Cable and Tigo run **$40-$70/month for 200-500 Mbps**, installed within a week if the building already has fiber wiring. In smaller towns like Boquete or Coronado, you're more often buying a cable or DSL-hybrid plan at **$35-$55/month for 15-50 Mbps**, and installation can take two to four weeks because technicians serve a wider radius with fewer crews.

### Starlink: the backup most remote workers now treat as primary

Starlink is fully available in Panama and has become the default fix for anyone whose rental sits outside a strong fiber zone. The standard kit costs **around $350-$400 upfront** plus **$60-$120/month** depending on the plan, and it is the single most common upgrade mentioned in expat housing groups for Bocas del Toro, Volcán, and rural Chiriquí. Several landlords in Boquete and coastal areas now include a Starlink dish as a listed amenity precisely because tenants ask for it before signing.

## What it actually costs to stay connected

Budget for connectivity as its own line item, not an afterthought folded into rent:

- Fiber/cable plan: **$35-$70/month**
- Starlink (if needed): **$60-$120/month** plus the one-time hardware cost
- Local SIM with data (Movistar, Tigo, Digicel): **$15-$30/month** for 10-20GB, useful as a phone-based hotspot backup
- Coworking day pass, as an emergency workspace: **$10-$20/day**

Most remote workers in fiber-covered areas spend around $50-$60/month total. Those in weaker-coverage towns often end up paying $100-$150/month once Starlink and a mobile backup are added in.

## Coworking spaces as insurance, not just amenity

Panama City, Boquete, and Coronado all now have dedicated coworking spaces with commercial-grade fiber and backup generators — useful not because a home office isn't viable, but because every connection eventually has a bad day. A day pass running $10-$20, or a monthly membership around $80-$150, is cheap insurance against the one outage that lands during a deadline. Treat the nearest coworking space the way you'd treat a backup generator: you hope not to need it, but you want to know exactly where it is before you need it.

## The outage problem nobody mentions upfront

Internet speed gets all the attention; power reliability gets almost none, despite mattering just as much. Outside Panama City's core, brief power outages of a few minutes to a couple of hours are common during the rainy season (roughly May through November), and they take your router, modem, and any wired connection down with them. A small UPS battery backup (**$50-$120**) keeps a router running through short outages, and it's one of the cheapest, least glamorous purchases that actually protects your ability to work.

## Panama's visa option built for this exact situation

Panama runs a dedicated [Digital Nomad Visa](/en/visas/digital-nomad/), aimed specifically at people earning foreign income while living in Panama. It requires proof of remote employment (or client letters if freelancing) and monthly income of at least $3,000, with processing taking one to two months and up to nine months of stay, renewable. It's a different track from the [Pensionado visa](/en/visas/pensionado/), which is built for retirees with a pension rather than active income, so pick the one that actually matches your situation rather than defaulting to whichever one you've heard of first.

## Before you sign a lease

Ask the landlord for the exact ISP and plan name, not just "fast internet included," and run a speed test from the unit itself if you can arrange a visit or ask a local contact to do it. If that's not possible, check whether Starlink is an option at the address before committing to a 12-month lease in a weak-coverage pocket. It's a five-minute question that prevents months of frustration.
