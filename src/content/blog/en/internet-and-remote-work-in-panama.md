A Zoom call dropped mid-sentence during a board meeting is the moment most new remote workers in Panama learn that "fiber available" and "fiber reliable" are two different claims. The internet here is good enough to run a business on — plenty of people do — but the infrastructure has gaps that don't show up in a speed test, and they matter more than the advertised Mbps number ever will.

Here's what remote work infrastructure actually looks like once you're living it instead of reading about it.

## Fiber coverage: where it exists and where it doesn't

Panama City, Panama Oeste (the western suburbs like Coronado and the Pacific corridor), and the main expat towns in Chiriquí (Boquete, David) all have fiber-to-the-home from at least one provider. Outside those corridors — small towns in the interior, more remote parts of Bocas del Toro, mountain areas past Boquete's center — you're often back to DSL or fixed wireless, and speeds drop fast.

Cable Onda and Tigo (Más Móvil) are the two major fiber providers, with Digicel also competing in some markets. In Panama City and Coronado, 100-300 Mbps fiber plans are standard and reasonably priced. In Boquete's town center, Cable Onda fiber reaches most addresses; a few minutes up the mountain toward Jaramillo or Volcancito, you may be stuck with wireless internet that caps out around 10-20 Mbps and gets worse when it rains.

| Area | Typical connection | Advertised speed | Monthly cost | Real-world reliability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Panama City (Casco, San Francisco, Costa del Este) | Fiber | 100-500 Mbps | $40-$80 | High |
| Coronado / Panama Oeste beach corridor | Fiber | 100-300 Mbps | $45-$75 | High |
| Boquete town center | Fiber | 50-200 Mbps | $40-$70 | Medium-high |
| Boquete outskirts / mountain roads | Fixed wireless | 10-20 Mbps | $35-$55 | Medium, weather-affected |
| Bocas del Toro (main island) | Fiber/cable | 50-150 Mbps | $45-$80 | Medium |
| Interior small towns (Santa Fe, Pedasí, etc.) | DSL/wireless | 5-30 Mbps | $30-$50 | Low-medium |

Before signing a lease anywhere outside Panama City or Coronado, ask the specific address's internet options — not the town's. Two houses on the same street can have completely different service because of where the fiber line physically ends.

## The real bottleneck: power, not bandwidth

Expats who've worked remotely from Panama for a year or more will tell you the connection isn't usually the problem — the power is. Rainy season (roughly May through November) brings scheduled and unscheduled outages, especially in Boquete, Bocas del Toro, and parts of the interior, where a hard rain can knock out power for anywhere from twenty minutes to several hours.

In Panama City this is rare; the grid there is far more stable. Outside the capital, it's common enough that anyone doing client calls for a living treats a UPS (uninterruptible power supply) as non-negotiable gear, not an upgrade. A mid-range UPS ($80-$150) keeps a router and laptop running through a short outage and gives you time to switch to a mobile hotspot if it runs longer. People serious about uptime also keep a second internet connection — usually a prepaid mobile data SIM from Tigo or Digicel as backup — so a fiber outage doesn't mean a missed deadline.

## Mobile data as a legitimate backup

Panama's mobile networks are genuinely good. 4G LTE covers nearly the entire populated country, and 5G is live in Panama City and expanding. A prepaid SIM with a data plan costs $15-$30/month for 10-20GB, and tethering a laptop to a phone hotspot is a normal fallback here, not a last resort. Many remote workers in smaller towns run mobile data as their primary backup and fiber as primary — or sometimes the reverse, depending on which one their specific street favors that month.

## Coworking spaces and cafes with real wifi

For anyone not working from a home office, or anyone who wants backup power and backup internet handled by someone else, coworking spaces have grown fast in the main expat hubs.

- **Panama City**: Several coworking spaces in San Francisco, Casco Viejo, and Costa del Este, from $120-$250/month for a dedicated desk, most with backup generators.
- **Boquete**: A handful of cafes and small coworking setups cater specifically to remote workers, with day passes around $8-$15 and monthly options closer to $80-$150.
- **Bocas del Toro**: Fewer formal spaces, but several hostels and cafes on Isla Colón offer decent wifi and a laptop-friendly atmosphere.

If your work depends on zero downtime — client calls, trading, livestreaming — a coworking space with its own generator is worth the monthly fee even if your home connection is fine nine days out of ten.

## What this means for visa planning

If remote work income is how you plan to support yourself in Panama, infrastructure reliability should factor into where you choose to live, not just cost of living or weather. This also intersects with how you structure your stay legally — many remote workers come in on a [Pensionado visa](/en/visas/pensionado) if they qualify through a pension, while others use short-term options while sorting out longer residency. Neither visa path requires employment in Panama, which is part of why remote work and Panama relocation go together so often — but it means your income depends entirely on infrastructure you don't control, so test it before you commit.

If you can, rent short-term in your target neighborhood for a month before signing a year-long lease, and run your actual workday from there — video calls, uploads, the works — before deciding it's home.
