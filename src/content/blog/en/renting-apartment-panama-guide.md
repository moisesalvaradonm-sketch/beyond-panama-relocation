Finding an apartment in Panama is not complicated, but it works differently than what most foreigners expect. Landlords have their own informal rules, many rental transactions happen outside formal agencies, and your credit history from your home country means nothing here. This guide covers the process as it actually works.

## What Documents Landlords Typically Ask For

Requirements vary by landlord and building type, but here is what you should have ready:

**For tourists and recent arrivals (no visa yet):**
- Passport (copy of photo page and entry stamp)
- Proof of income: bank statements from your last 3–6 months, ideally showing a regular deposit or a balance of at least 3× the monthly rent
- Reference letters: a previous landlord or employer helps; if you have neither, a personal letter explaining your situation can work

**For residents with a visa:**
- Cedula (Panamanian ID card) or valid residency card
- Bank statements or proof of pension/income
- RUC (tax ID) if you have one — some landlords request it, most don't

**For employed applicants:**
- Employment letter from your company in Panama
- Last 3 pay stubs

The biggest surprise for most foreigners: **Panama does not run credit checks the way North American landlords do.** The landlord's main concern is whether you can pay and whether you seem reliable. A solid bank statement goes a long way.

## Security Deposits and Upfront Costs

In Panama, the legal maximum security deposit is **one month's rent**. In practice:

- Most landlords ask for 1 month deposit + 1 month advance rent (so 2 months upfront at signing)
- Furnished apartments sometimes ask for 1.5–2 months deposit, especially if the furnishings are high-value
- Some buildings (especially condo towers) charge a building move-in fee separately, typically $150–$300

Ask about the building's move-in fee before signing. It is almost always non-refundable and paid to the building administration, not the landlord.

## Furnished vs. Unfurnished: What to Expect

**Furnished** in Panama typically means:
- Bed, sofa, dining table, basic kitchen appliances (fridge, stove, microwave)
- Air conditioners in bedrooms and living room
- Sometimes a washer; rarely a dryer
- Basic cookware and dishes vary — always ask or visit

**Unfurnished** means bare walls. No appliances, often no ceiling fixtures. Factor in $2,000–$5,000 to equip a one-bedroom apartment from scratch if you are starting with nothing.

A middle option is increasingly common: **semi-furnished**, which usually includes appliances and A/C but not furniture.

## How to Find Rentals

**Online platforms:**
- **Encuentra24.com** — the largest classified site in Panama; most landlords list here
- **OLX Panama** — second-largest, more individual landlords
- **Facebook Marketplace and Facebook groups** — surprisingly active; search "Apartamentos en Alquiler Panamá"
- **MetroCuadrado.com** — more formal listings, often from agencies
- **Airbnb** — useful for short stays (1–3 months) while you search; rates are higher but no long-term commitment

**Real estate agencies:**
Agents in Panama typically charge **one month's rent as commission**, paid by the tenant. Some charge half a month; a few list as "no commission." If you use an agent, clarify the fee structure before viewing properties.

**Word of mouth:**
Many of the best apartments in Panama never get listed publicly. They circulate through social networks, expat Facebook groups, and Whatsapp chains. The expat community in Panama is small enough that personal referrals matter.

## Understanding the Lease Contract

Panama lease contracts are governed by Law 93 of 1973 and its amendments. Key points:

- **Minimum term:** There is no legally mandated minimum, but most landlords require 6–12 months
- **Rent increases:** Landlords can only raise rent at lease renewal, not mid-term, unless the contract specifies otherwise
- **Termination by tenant:** Most contracts require 30–60 days written notice; verify this in your contract
- **Termination by landlord:** A landlord can only evict for cause (non-payment, damage, etc.) or for personal use of the property, with proper legal process
- **Utilities:** Confirm what is included. In most apartments, the tenant pays electricity, water, and internet separately. In some buildings, water is included in the HOA fee — ask

If the landlord or agency only offers a contract in Spanish and you do not read Spanish well, hire a lawyer to review it. This costs $100–$200 and is worth it.

## Common Traps to Avoid

**1. "Administration fee" confusion**
Many condo buildings charge a monthly HOA fee (cuota de mantenimiento), typically $100–$300/month. Sometimes this is included in the rent, sometimes not. Clarify in writing.

**2. Undisclosed water damage or A/C issues**
Air conditioning is essential in Panama's climate. Test every unit before signing. Note any moisture stains on walls or ceilings — water infiltration from upper floors is a common problem in older buildings.

**3. Verbal agreements**
Panama has a strong tradition of verbal agreements, and both parties often feel bound by them. Get everything in writing regardless.

**4. Unlicensed agents**
Real estate agents in Panama are not required by law to be licensed, though the profession is regulated. Anyone can call themselves an agent. If using an agency, verify they are established and can provide references.

**5. Rent in USD vs. Balboas**
Panama's currency is the Balboa, tied 1:1 to the US dollar. All rents are effectively in USD. There is no exchange risk.

## Renting Without a Visa: Is It Possible?

Yes. You do not need a visa or residency to rent an apartment in Panama. Tourists with a valid passport can sign lease agreements. The limitation is practical, not legal: landlords want to verify that you will stay and can pay. If you are on a tourist visa and plan to extend through the visa renewal process (exit the country every 90–180 days), some landlords will be cautious. Having a strong bank statement and being upfront about your situation is the most effective approach.

## Practical Next Steps

1. **Determine your budget** including utilities (electricity in Panama runs $80–$200/month for a 1BR with A/C, depending on usage)
2. **Choose your zone** based on proximity to your work, lifestyle, and budget
3. **Gather your documents** before starting your search — it speeds up the process considerably
4. **Visit multiple apartments in person** — listing photos in Panama are often generous
5. **Negotiate** — especially in slower seasons (May–August), landlords are often willing to reduce price or include parking
