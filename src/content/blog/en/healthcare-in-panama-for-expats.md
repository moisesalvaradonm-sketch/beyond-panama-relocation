A hip replacement that costs $40,000 in the United States runs about $12,000-$16,000 at Hospital Punta Pacífica in Panama City, with the same Johns Hopkins-affiliated standards of care. That price gap is the first thing most expats learn about Panamanian healthcare, and it's also the thing that gets oversimplified into "healthcare is cheap here" — which isn't quite true once you factor in insurance, location, and what happens when something goes wrong outside the capital.

Here's what the system actually looks like, what it costs, and where it falls short.

## The two-tier system: public vs. private

Panama runs two parallel healthcare systems, and which one you'll use depends almost entirely on where you live and what you can pay.

The public system, run by the Caja de Seguro Social (CSS), serves Panamanian citizens and legal residents who pay into social security. Coverage is essentially free at the point of use, but wait times for non-emergency procedures can stretch months, and rural clinics are often understaffed. Most expats who qualify for CSS keep it as a backup rather than a primary option.

The private system is what almost every expat actually uses. It's concentrated in Panama City, with a second tier in David (Chiriquí) and smaller private clinics in expat hubs like Boquete, Coronado, and Bocas del Toro. Private hospitals in the capital are genuinely world-class — several have accreditation or affiliation agreements with U.S. institutions, English-speaking doctors trained in the U.S. or Europe, and modern equipment.

The catch: outside Panama City and David, private care thins out fast. Boquete has good general practitioners and urgent care, but anything serious — a cardiac event, major surgery, cancer treatment — means a drive or flight to the capital. If you're choosing a retirement town based on climate and rent alone, check the distance to a Level I hospital before you sign a lease.

## What a hospital visit actually costs

| Service | Panama City private hospital | Typical U.S. equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| Doctor consultation (specialist) | $40-$80 | $150-$300 |
| Emergency room visit (non-admitted) | $150-$400 | $1,200-$3,000 |
| MRI scan | $350-$600 | $1,500-$4,000 |
| Normal delivery (childbirth) | $3,500-$6,000 | $13,000-$20,000 |
| Hip replacement surgery | $12,000-$16,000 | $30,000-$50,000 |
| Appendectomy | $4,000-$7,000 | $15,000-$30,000 |

These are cash-pay prices at top facilities like Hospital Punta Pacífica, Hospital Nacional, or Pacífica Salud. Clinics in smaller towns run 30-50% cheaper for routine visits, but complex procedures still funnel back to the capital.

## Health insurance: what foreigners actually pay

Private health insurance in Panama is not optional if you want to avoid the full cash-pay bill for a major event, and it's also not as cheap as the consultation prices above might suggest.

Local insurers — ASSA, Pan-American Life (PALIG), and Mapfre are the three most used by expats — price premiums primarily on age and pre-existing conditions, not on income. A healthy 45-year-old typically pays $120-$220/month for solid coverage with a moderate deductible. A healthy 65-year-old is usually looking at $250-$450/month. Past 70, premiums climb sharply and some insurers cap new enrollments around age 70-75, which catches a lot of retirees off guard when they try to switch providers later in life.

Pre-existing conditions are the real variable. Local insurers routinely exclude coverage for a known condition for the first 1-2 years, or decline certain applicants outright. This is the single biggest reason expats arrive healthy, buy a policy immediately, and never let the coverage lapse — switching insurers later with a new diagnosis on record is far harder and more expensive than staying put.

International health insurance (Cigna Global, IMG, Allianz Care) is the other common route, especially for people who want coverage that also works when they travel back to the U.S. or Europe. It's considerably more expensive — often $300-$700+/month depending on age and coverage region — but it skips Panama's local pre-existing-condition exclusions in many cases and gives broader network access.

If you're applying for the [Pensionado visa](/en/visas/pensionado), note that Panama does not require proof of health insurance for that visa category, but every insurance broker and most financial advisors in Panama will tell you not to rely on that as a reason to skip coverage.

## The CSS option for residents

If you obtain Panamanian residency and later qualify to pay into the CSS system — which generally requires either formal employment or voluntary enrollment in specific cases — you get access to public hospitals and a much lower cost structure. It's a slower, more bureaucratic system, and English is not guaranteed at public facilities. Most expats we've spoken with use CSS as a supplement for routine, low-stakes care (a basic lab test, a prescription refill) while keeping private insurance for anything serious.

## Pharmacies and prescriptions

Pharmacies are plentiful and well-stocked in Panama City, David, and the larger expat towns, and many common medications that require a prescription in the U.S. are available over the counter in Panama — blood pressure medication, some antibiotics, and various maintenance drugs among them. Brand-name drugs cost roughly 20-40% less than U.S. retail prices; generics are often dramatically cheaper. Bring a 90-day supply of anything unusual when you first arrive, just to give yourself time to find the equivalent locally.

## Dental and vision

Dental care deserves a specific mention because it's one of the most dramatic price gaps in the system. A crown that runs $1,200-$2,000 in the U.S. costs $300-$500 in Panama City, performed by dentists trained to the same standards. This has created a small but real "dental tourism" flow of people who fly in specifically for major dental work, factoring the flight into their savings and still coming out ahead. Vision care follows a similar pattern — routine exams and glasses are inexpensive, and LASIK runs a fraction of U.S. pricing at reputable clinics in the capital.

## What to actually do before you move

Get a private policy quoted and bound before you need it, not after. Confirm which hospital your prospective insurer has direct billing agreements with, since some policies require you to pay upfront and get reimbursed if you use an out-of-network facility — a meaningful difference when the bill is $12,000. And if you're settling outside Panama City, drive the route to the nearest Level I hospital once, in daylight, before you decide the location works for you long-term.
