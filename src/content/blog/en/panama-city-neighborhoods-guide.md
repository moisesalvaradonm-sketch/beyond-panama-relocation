If you ask five expats where to live in Panama City, you'll get five different answers. That's not because any of them are wrong — it's because this city has genuinely distinct neighborhoods that suit very different personalities and budgets.

This guide walks through the main options. No fluff, no developer-speak. Just an honest take on what each neighborhood is actually like to live in.

## A quick note on the city's layout

Panama City runs east to west along the Pacific coast, with the old historic center (Casco Viejo) on the western end and the newer, wealthier districts extending east. The canal is to the north and west. You'll hear people refer to "the strip" — that's the dense ribbon of high-rises along the bay known as the Cinta Costera.

When choosing a neighborhood, keep in mind: Panama has two seasons. The dry season runs roughly December through April — warm, sunny, virtually no rain. The rainy season is May through November — sudden heavy downpours every afternoon, humidity that makes certain areas feel subtropical. Some neighborhoods flood; some don't. That detail matters more than most blog posts admit.

## Punta Pacífica / Punta Paitilla

**Who it's for:** Professionals, retirees who want convenience, anyone who likes modern infrastructure and doesn't want to think about logistics.

These two adjacent neighborhoods are the closest Panama City gets to a self-contained luxury zone. High-rise apartments with ocean or bay views, Hospital Punta Pacifica (a Johns Hopkins affiliate) within walking distance, good restaurants and a few quality supermarkets nearby.

The tradeoff is that it's dense, parking is a fight, and street-level life is limited. You live in your building more than in the neighborhood. Socially, it skews toward wealthy Panamanians, expats on corporate packages, and diplomats.

**Rent:** $1,500–$4,000/month for a 2-bedroom. The range is wide — older towers on the Paitilla side can be $1,500 fully furnished; new construction with views and amenities in Punta Pacífica will push $3,500+.

## San Francisco

**Who it's for:** Expats who want a real neighborhood feel without sacrificing access to good food, gyms, and international supermarkets.

San Francisco is probably the best value-for-quality option for most expats. It's centrally located, walkable by Panama City standards, and has a genuine mix of local Panamanian life and expat-friendly infrastructure.

You'll find good coffee shops, independent restaurants, the large Via España commercial corridor, and a few parks. Riba Smith supermarket (the best chain in the city) has a large branch there. The Multiplaza mall is a short ride away.

Flooding is occasional in some streets during heavy rainy season downpours, but nothing extreme in the higher-elevation pockets of the neighborhood.

**Rent:** $800–$2,000/month for a 2-bedroom. Older furnished apartments closer to $800–$1,100; newer buildings with amenities push $1,500–$2,000.

## El Cangrejo

**Who it's for:** Budget-conscious expats, people who want to walk everywhere, anyone who values character over polish.

El Cangrejo is one of the older expat neighborhoods in the city, and it shows — both in its charm and its age. The buildings are mostly mid-century, the streets are walkable and tree-lined, and there's a dense concentration of local restaurants, small shops, and café-style spots that few other neighborhoods can match.

It's not glamorous. The building stock is dated, some streets are rough around the edges, and you won't get the bay views or the resort-style amenities. But you will get a neighborhood where you can walk to get groceries, a coffee, a haircut, and a decent meal without getting in a car.

Via Argentina, El Cangrejo's main commercial street, is genuinely pleasant. The proximity to Bella Vista and San Francisco means you can walk or take a short taxi to most things.

**Rent:** $700–$1,500/month for a 2-bedroom. One of the more affordable central options.

## Costa del Este

**Who it's for:** Families, people who want space, anyone who prefers the quiet suburban feel and doesn't mind depending on a car.

Costa del Este is as close as Panama City gets to an American suburb. Wide roads, gated communities, manicured landscaping, large houses and apartments, good international schools nearby. It's quiet, safe, and feels very planned — because it is.

The catch: you need a car for everything. There's no real street life, no walking to the corner store. The commute into central Panama City can be slow during rush hours, especially if you're heading west toward Miraflores or the business district.

During the rainy season, some lower-lying streets in Costa del Este have had flooding issues. Check specific areas before committing.

**Rent:** $1,200–$3,000/month for a 2-bedroom house or large apartment. Gated communities with full amenities sit at the higher end.

---

## Casco Viejo / Casco Antiguo

**Who it's for:** Young professionals, remote workers, people who love historic cities and don't mind the noise and energy of an urban core.

Casco Viejo is Panama City's UNESCO-listed historic district, and over the past decade it's gone through a serious renovation. Colonial buildings that were crumbling shells are now boutique hotels, restaurants, and loft apartments. The rooftop views of the canal entrance and the bay are legitimately stunning.

Living there is genuinely cool — and also genuinely challenging. Noise from bars and restaurants on weekends is real. Some blocks are fully restored; others are still mid-renovation. Parking is difficult. Street flooding in certain corners can be significant during rainy season.

The expat community here skews younger and tends toward remote workers, creative types, and people who specifically chose the urban-historic atmosphere over convenience.

**Rent:** $1,000–$2,500/month for a 1–2 bedroom. Renovated lofts in converted historic buildings command a premium; older unrenovated spaces can be cheaper but have tradeoffs.

---

## Marbella / Bella Vista

**Who it's for:** Expats who want to be central without the premium of Punta Pacífica or the noise of Casco.

Marbella and Bella Vista form a transition zone between the high-end eastern districts and the older center of the city. The neighborhood is a mix of residential towers, older apartment buildings, embassies, and a decent local commercial strip.

It's a solid choice for people who want to be close to everything without paying top dollar. The quality of specific buildings varies considerably, so viewing apartments in person matters more here than in newer neighborhoods.

**Rent:** $900–$2,000/month for a 2-bedroom.

## Practical notes before you decide

**Weather and flooding matter more than real estate listings admit.** Before committing to any neighborhood, look up whether specific streets or buildings have flooding history during rainy season. Ask locals, not just the landlord.

**Visit before you sign.** This is the most consistent advice from expats who've been here a few years. Walk the neighborhood at different times — a Tuesday morning versus a Saturday night will give you a very different picture.

**Furnished apartments dominate the expat market.** Most landlords in expat-facing buildings offer fully furnished units at a premium. If you're arriving with furniture or planning to stay long-term, negotiating an unfurnished rate can save $300–$500/month.

**Proximity to your work or social life matters.** Panama City traffic can be brutal during rush hours. A 5 km commute can take 45 minutes. Factor in where you'll actually spend your time.

**The first apartment is rarely the last.** Most expats move once after their first year — they choose somewhere that sounds good on paper, then find their actual rhythm and relocate accordingly. That's fine. Pick a furnished apartment with a 6-month lease and give yourself space to learn.
