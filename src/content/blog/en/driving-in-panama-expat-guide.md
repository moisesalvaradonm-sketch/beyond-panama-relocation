A foreign tourist license in Panama is valid for 90 days from entry — not 90 days per trip, and not renewable by leaving and re-entering the country the way some old forums claim. Immigration officials have cracked down on "visa runs" used to extend a foreign license's validity, and police checkpoints now routinely check entry stamps against the date on your license. Get this wrong and you're driving unlicensed, which voids your insurance in a crash.

That single fact trips up more new arrivals than any other part of driving here. This guide covers what actually governs driving in Panama as a foreigner: when you need a local license, how traffic enforcement really works, what a car costs to buy and insure, and the mistakes that cost people money or worse.

## When your foreign license stops working

Tourists and visitors on a 90-day entry stamp can drive on a valid foreign license (plus an International Driving Permit is recommended, though not always checked) for those 90 days. Once you have residency — even provisional residency — the rules change:

- **Provisional residency holders**: can usually continue driving on a foreign license for a limited window, but this is inconsistently enforced and increasingly checked.
- **Permanent residents**: are legally required to obtain a Panamanian driver's license. Driving long-term on a foreign license as a permanent resident is a real legal exposure, not a technicality.

To get a Panamanian license, you'll go through the Autoridad del Tránsito y Transporte Terrestre (ATTT). If you hold a valid license from a country with a reciprocity agreement with Panama (the U.S., Canada, most EU countries, and several Latin American nations qualify), you can usually convert it without retaking the driving test — just a vision exam, paperwork, notarized translation if your license isn't in Spanish, and a fee. Without a qualifying license, you'll need to complete lessons at a certified driving school and sit both the written and practical exams.

Budget **2-4 weeks** for the conversion process if your paperwork is in order, longer if you need document translations or apostilles.

## How traffic enforcement actually works

Panama's traffic rules on paper look familiar to anyone who's driven in North America or Europe: right-hand drive, metric speed limits, mandatory seatbelts, child seats required for young children. In practice, enforcement is uneven and driving culture is more assertive than many newcomers expect.

A few things that surprise expats:

- **Lane discipline is loose.** Lane markings are treated as suggestions in heavy traffic, especially in Panama City and on the Interamericana highway. Expect vehicles to merge with little signaling.
- **Checkpoints are common**, particularly at night and around holidays. Police check license, registration, and insurance (SOAT — the mandatory basic liability policy). Have physical copies, not just photos on your phone — some officers insist on originals.
- **Fines are usually paid later, not on the spot.** Panama moved away from on-the-spot cash payments to reduce bribery; tickets are typically processed through the ATTT system and paid online or at a bank. If an officer demands immediate cash payment with no paperwork, that's a red flag.
- **Right of way at unmarked intersections** defaults to whoever is already in the intersection or arrives from the right, but in practice, the more assertive driver often goes first. Defensive driving, not rule-following, is what keeps you safe.

## Buying a car as a foreigner

You do not need residency to buy a car in Panama, but you do need a *cédula* (national ID) or passport plus a RUC (tax ID number, which foreigners can obtain) to register a vehicle in your name. Dealerships and private sellers both routinely sell to foreigners, including tourists in some cases, though registration logistics are simpler once you have at least provisional residency.

| Vehicle type | New price range | Used price range (3-5 years old) |
|---|---|---|
| Compact sedan (Hyundai Accent, Kia Rio) | $18,000 - $24,000 | $9,000 - $14,000 |
| Midsize SUV (Toyota RAV4, Hyundai Tucson) | $28,000 - $38,000 | $16,000 - $24,000 |
| Pickup truck (Toyota Hilux, Ford Ranger) | $32,000 - $45,000 | $18,000 - $28,000 |
| 4x4 for mountain/rural areas (Toyota Fortuner, Land Cruiser) | $45,000 - $70,000 | $25,000 - $40,000 |

New car prices in Panama run noticeably higher than in the U.S. for equivalent trims, mostly due to import taxes (which can add 15-25% depending on vehicle type and origin). This is why a large share of the expat community, especially in Boquete and the Azuero Peninsula, buys used vehicles from departing expats or local dealers rather than ordering new.

**Import taxes also mean bringing your own car from home is usually not worth it.** Shipping costs, import duties, and the paperwork to register a foreign-titled vehicle often exceed what you'd save, and the process can take months. Most relocation guides and most expats who've actually done it recommend selling before you move and buying locally instead.

## Insurance: what's mandatory and what you actually need

**SOAT** (Seguro Obligatorio de Accidentes de Tránsito) is the legally required minimum — basic liability coverage for injury to others in an accident you cause. It's inexpensive, typically **$75-$150/year**, but the coverage limits are low and it does not cover damage to your own vehicle.

Most foreign residents carry additional **full coverage (cobertura amplia)** insurance, which covers collision, theft, and often includes roadside assistance. Expect to pay:

| Coverage level | Typical annual cost |
|---|---|
| SOAT only (minimum legal) | $75 - $150 |
| Full coverage, compact/midsize car | $500 - $900 |
| Full coverage, SUV/truck | $700 - $1,300 |

Given how common fender-benders and theft of parts (mirrors, wheels, catalytic converters) are reported to be in urban areas, most expats consider full coverage non-negotiable, not optional.

## Driving outside the city: what changes

Road quality drops noticeably once you're off the Pan-American Highway and into rural or mountain areas. Roads to parts of Bocas del Toro, the Darién approach, and mountain routes around Boquete and Volcán can be unpaved, poorly lit, and prone to landslides in rainy season (roughly May through November). A 4x4 or AWD vehicle isn't just a preference in these areas — for some routes during heavy rain, it's the only practical option.

Flooding on low-lying roads is also a recurring rainy-season hazard. Locals generally know which roads flood and avoid them after heavy rain; new arrivals don't, and vehicle write-offs from flood damage are common enough that insurers ask about it.

## The bottom line

Getting your Panamanian license sorted early, carrying full insurance, and buying used rather than importing are the three decisions that save new arrivals the most money and the most legal headaches. None of this is complicated once you know the rules — it's mostly a matter of not assuming driving here works exactly like it did back home.
