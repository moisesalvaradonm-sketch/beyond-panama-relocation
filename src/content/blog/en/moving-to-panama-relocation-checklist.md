Moving to a new country is organized chaos at best. Panama is no exception — but it's more manageable than most because the infrastructure is solid, the dollar makes finances simple, and the relocation industry is mature enough that you're not figuring everything out from scratch.

This checklist covers what actually matters, roughly in the order you'll need to do it. Skip the things that don't apply to your situation.

## 6–12 Months Before You Move

### Decide on Your Visa Category

Panama has several paths to legal residency, and the right one depends on your income source, nationality, and long-term plans. The main options:

- **Friendly Nations Visa** — Citizens of 50+ countries who can demonstrate economic ties to Panama (job offer, company formation, or investment). This is the most flexible path and processes in 3–6 months.
- **Pensionado Visa** — $1,000/month in pension income from a government or private pension. Comes with substantial discounts on medical, restaurants, hotels, and entertainment.
- **Digital Nomad Visa** — $3,000/month in verifiable remote income from non-Panamanian sources.
- **Qualified Investor** — $300,000+ in real estate or business investment.

Start early. Even Panama's faster visa programs take several months, and you'll need apostilled documents from your home country — a process that can itself take 4–8 weeks.

### Get Your Documents Apostilled

Whatever visa you pursue, you'll need apostilled copies of:

- Birth certificate
- Police clearance from your home country (and any country you've lived in for 5+ years)
- Marriage certificate (if applicable)
- Proof of income documents (bank statements, pension letters, employer verification)

An apostille is a certification that makes your documents legally recognized internationally. In the US, apostilles are issued by the Secretary of State's office in the state where the document was issued. Processing times vary from 2 days (some states offer expedited) to 8 weeks. Factor this in early.

### Hire a Panamanian Immigration Lawyer

This is not optional. Panama's immigration system is manageable with professional help and significantly more frustrating without it. A good immigration lawyer will:

- Review your documents before submission to avoid rejections
- Navigate the paperwork on your behalf (most of which is in Spanish)
- Know the current processing times and any recent changes to requirements
- Help you understand your rights and obligations as a resident

Expect to pay $1,500–$3,000 in legal fees depending on the visa type, not including government fees. Get referrals from expat forums or relocation services rather than picking randomly.

### Research Neighborhoods Seriously

Panama City has dramatically different neighborhoods that suit different lifestyles:

- **Miraflores / San Francisco / Obarrio** — established expat areas, good infrastructure, walkable, well-served by restaurants and services
- **Costa del Este / Santa María** — newer, more suburban, popular with families, excellent amenities
- **Casco Viejo** — historic district, beautiful colonial architecture, increasingly gentrified, good for younger expats or those without cars
- **Punta Pacífica / Punta Paitilla** — high-rise living with bay views, walkable to Panama City's best hospitals
- **Clayton** — former US military zone, spacious houses, good schools nearby, more suburban feel

If you're not moving to Panama City, Boquete (cool mountain climate, established expat community) and the Azuero Peninsula (slower pace, lower cost) are the main alternatives.

Visit before you commit if possible. A neighborhood that reads well on paper can feel different in person — traffic patterns, noise levels, and proximity to what you actually need matter more than a description.

## 3–6 Months Before You Move

### Decide What to Ship vs. What to Buy There

Shipping to Panama is possible but expensive. Duties run 10–25% on most goods, and furniture and appliances are subject to customs fees. Electronics can be imported duty-free up to a certain value, but that threshold changes.

What usually makes sense to ship:
- Sentimental items that can't be replaced
- High-end equipment (professional tools, musical instruments)
- Books (if you have a significant collection)
- Your car (if it's less than 3 years old and low mileage — older cars face higher duties)

What usually makes sense to buy in Panama:
- Furniture (Panama has good options at various price points)
- Appliances (major brands are available, though at a premium)
- General household goods

If you're a Pensionado visa holder, you get a one-time exemption to import household goods duty-free — one of the most valuable practical benefits of that visa.

### Start the Bank Account Process Early

This is one of the things expats most underestimate. Opening a bank account in Panama as a non-resident is difficult. As a resident with a cedula (Panamanian ID), it becomes straightforward — but you won't have your cedula on day one.

Options while you wait for residency:
- Some banks (Multibank, Global Bank) are more flexible about opening accounts for people in the residency process, particularly with a letter from your immigration lawyer
- Correspondent banking: maintain your home country account and transfer as needed
- Some US banks (Schwab, Charles Schwab Investor Checking) offer zero-fee international ATM withdrawals, which is useful in the interim

As a legal resident, you'll be able to open accounts at most major banks without significant issues. Required documents typically include your cedula, proof of income, and sometimes a reference letter.

### Sort Out Health Insurance

Panama's private healthcare is good and significantly cheaper than the US, but it's not free. Your options:

- **International health insurance** — covers you in Panama and globally, useful if you travel frequently or might return to your home country for care. Expect $200–$600/month depending on age and coverage.
- **Local Panamanian insurance** — cheaper, covers you primarily in Panama. Several good local providers (ASSA, Nacional de Seguros, Blue Cross Panama).
- **Self-pay** — if you're generally healthy and have savings, many expats in their 40s–50s self-pay for routine care (which is inexpensive) and maintain catastrophic coverage only.

Don't arrive without something in place.

## 1–3 Months Before You Move

### Find Temporary Housing First

Don't sign a long-term lease before you've lived in a neighborhood. Most expats spend 1–3 months in furnished short-term rentals (available on Airbnb, Trova Trip, or directly from owners) before committing to a permanent place.

A furnished one-bedroom in a good Panama City neighborhood runs $1,200–$2,000/month short-term, often including utilities. It's worth the premium to avoid being locked into the wrong location.

### Transfer Money Strategically

Large international wire transfers are the standard way to move money to Panama. Panama's banks accept these routinely. Things to know:

- Transfers over $10,000 to Panama require source of funds documentation — have your bank statements ready to show the money's origin
- Wire fees range from $15–$50 per transfer
- Services like Wise (TransferWise) work for smaller amounts and can be more cost-effective
- Panama uses the US dollar, so no currency conversion if you're coming from the US

Avoid arriving with large amounts of cash. Panama's banks are accustomed to incoming wire transfers from relocating expats.

### Plan Your Pet Relocation

If you're bringing pets, start early — this is one of the most bureaucratically intensive parts of the move.

Requirements to bring pets to Panama:
- Health certificate issued by a licensed vet within 2 weeks of travel
- Rabies vaccination (valid, not too recent or expired)
- Internal and external parasite treatment within 2 weeks of travel
- Certificate of origin / USDA-endorsed health certificate (for US residents)

Airlines have strict rules about animals in cargo vs. cabin. If your pet is small enough to travel in cabin, that's significantly less stressful. For larger dogs, research airlines with the best cargo policies — some suspend pet cargo in summer months due to heat.

## After You Arrive

### Get Your Cedula

Your cedula is your Panamanian national ID card. Once your residency is approved, getting your cedula is one of the first things to do. You'll need it for:

- Opening a bank account
- Signing a lease
- Getting a local phone plan
- Accessing Pensionado discounts (if applicable)
- Setting up utilities
- Almost everything else that requires ID

The cedula is issued at the Tribunal Electoral offices in Panama. Your immigration lawyer should guide you through this process, which normally takes 2–6 weeks after your residency card is issued.

### Get a Local Phone Plan

Options in Panama: Claro, Digicel, Tigo, and Movistar. Coverage varies by area — Claro and Tigo have the most robust national coverage.

Monthly plans for unlimited local calls and data run $25–$50. Bring an unlocked phone or buy one locally (imported phones at duty prices are available in the Melo and Panafoto chains).

### Register with Your Country's Embassy

Many countries recommend registering with their embassy when you move abroad. This lets the embassy contact you in emergencies, assists with document services, and puts you on notification lists for safety advisories. It's free, takes 10 minutes online, and is worth doing.

For Americans: the Smart Traveler Enrollment Program (STEP) at step.state.gov.

### Understand Your Tax Obligations at Home

Moving to Panama doesn't automatically end your tax obligations in your home country. Key points:

**Americans**: The IRS taxes US citizens on worldwide income regardless of where they live. You still file a US return every year. You may qualify for the Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (FEIE) or the Foreign Tax Credit, which can reduce or eliminate what you owe — but the filing obligation remains.

**Canadians**: Canada taxes by residency. If you sever residential ties (sell your home, close accounts, move your family), you can cease to be a Canadian tax resident. If you maintain ties, you likely remain taxable.

**EU citizens**: Varies significantly by country. Germany and Netherlands have exit taxes. Portugal and Greece have special expat tax regimes. Get country-specific advice before you leave.

### Find Your People

Panama has a large, active expat community. The resources that actually help:

- **Expat Exchange Panama** and **Internations Panama** — both active online communities
- **Facebook groups**: "Expats in Panama", "Gringos in Panama" — chaotic but useful for real-time advice
- **Boquete Community** groups if you're heading to the highlands
- Your immigration lawyer's network — good immigration attorneys often introduce clients to each other

Adjustment takes time. Most expats who make it past the first year say the hardest part was the first three months — learning systems, finding service providers, building routine. Give yourself that runway before judging whether you've made the right choice.

## The Things Nobody Tells You

**Things move differently here.** Government offices, bank appointments, and contractor timelines operate on a different clock. Build float into your plans.

**Customer service norms are different.** Not worse, different. Patience is a practical skill, not just a virtue.

**The heat is real.** If you haven't lived in a tropical climate, the humidity and temperature of Panama City will take adjustment. The interior regions and the highlands are cooler, which is why Boquete attracts a specific kind of expat.

**Panama City is genuinely international.** The quality of restaurants, cultural events, shopping, and professional services is higher than most newcomers expect from a Central American city. This surprises almost everyone.

**You will love some things and hate others.** The people who thrive in Panama are usually the ones who decided which things they'd accept before they got there, rather than discovering them as unwelcome surprises.
