There's a claim that circulates in expat forums: "Move to Panama and pay no taxes." Like most things on the internet, it's a simplification that gets people into trouble. The truth is more interesting — and more useful.

Panama operates on a **territorial tax system**. That means Panama only taxes income that is generated *within* Panama. If your money comes from outside the country — remote work for a foreign company, dividends from foreign stocks, rental income from a property in another country, a U.S. Social Security check, a pension from Germany — Panama does not touch it.

This isn't a loophole. It's how the Panamanian tax code was written, and it's been this way for decades.

## How Territorial Taxation Works

The key distinction is where income is *sourced*, not where you *live*.

Under Panama's system:
- **Foreign-sourced income** = not taxable in Panama
- **Panamanian-sourced income** = taxable at rates up to 25%

For most expats who retire here, work remotely for non-Panamanian employers, or manage investments abroad, the practical result is that their primary income stream faces no Panamanian income tax.

### What counts as Panamanian-sourced income?

- Salary or fees paid by a Panamanian company for work done in Panama
- Rental income from property located in Panama
- Business profits from operations conducted in Panama
- Interest from Panamanian bank deposits (subject to a 10% withholding tax)

If you open a local business, rent out a Panamanian condo, or take a job with a Panamanian employer, that income is subject to local tax.

## Who Benefits Most

### Remote Workers and Digital Nomads

If you work remotely for a company headquartered outside Panama — a U.S. tech firm, a European NGO, a Latin American startup — and your clients are also outside Panama, your salary is foreign-sourced. Panama doesn't tax it.

Panama introduced a **Digital Nomad Visa** in 2021 specifically for this profile. To qualify, you need to prove at least $3,000/month in remote income and show that your employer or clients are outside Panama.

One important note: **your home country may still tax you**. U.S. citizens, for example, are taxed on worldwide income regardless of where they live. Territorial tax in Panama doesn't override the IRS. If you're American, you still need to file — you just may qualify for the Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (FEIE), which for 2026 lets you exclude roughly $126,500 in earned foreign income.

### Retirees with Foreign Pensions and Investments

This is where Panama's tax treatment is particularly attractive. A retiree receiving:
- A U.S. Social Security benefit
- A private pension from a previous employer
- Dividends from a U.S. brokerage account
- Rental income from a property back home

...owes Panama nothing on any of it.

Panama doesn't require retirees to file a local tax return if their only income is foreign-sourced and they have no Panamanian business activity.

### Investors with Offshore Portfolios

Panama imposes no tax on capital gains from the sale of foreign securities. If you sell stock in Apple, Berkshire, or an index fund held in a U.S. brokerage, Panama doesn't tax the gain.

Compare that to many European countries, where capital gains taxes of 20–30% apply regardless of where the asset is held.

## What Panama Does Tax

To be clear-eyed about this, Panama does levy taxes. It's just that most expats aren't affected by them because their income doesn't originate here.

**Income tax on Panamanian-sourced income:**
- Up to $11,000: 0%
- $11,001 to $50,000: 15%
- Over $50,000: 25%

**Other taxes expats may encounter:**
- **ITBMS** (VAT): 7% on most goods and services purchased in Panama
- **Property transfer tax**: 2% when buying real estate
- **Annual property tax**: 0% on primary residences valued under $120,000; 0.5%–0.7% on higher values (with exemptions for new constructions for up to 20 years)
- **Import duties**: 10–25% on vehicles and certain goods brought into Panama

None of these are income taxes, and most are comparable to or lower than rates in Europe, Canada, or the U.S.

## The Panama Foundation (Fundación de Interés Privado)

More sophisticated expat investors sometimes establish a Panamanian Private Interest Foundation (PIF) to hold foreign assets. The PIF is a legal structure — not a trust, not a corporation — that can hold bank accounts, real estate, shares, and other assets.

Income generated by assets held *within* the PIF from *outside* Panama remains foreign-sourced and is not taxed by Panama. The PIF also offers estate planning benefits: assets held in it generally pass to beneficiaries without going through probate.

Setting up a PIF costs roughly $1,500–$3,000 in legal and registration fees, plus annual maintenance. It makes sense for people with complex asset structures or significant estates, not for most retirees.

## What You Still Need to Think About

### Your Home Country's Rules

Panama's territorial tax benefits don't eliminate your obligations to your country of origin. Key considerations:

**U.S. citizens and Green Card holders:** The IRS taxes worldwide income. You must file a U.S. tax return every year regardless of where you live. You may owe nothing if your income is below thresholds or you qualify for the FEIE, but the filing requirement doesn't go away.

**EU citizens:** Rules vary dramatically by country. Germany, France, and the Netherlands have exit taxes and may continue to tax residents who retain ties to their home country. Some countries (Portugal, Spain, Greece) have special regimes for returned expats. Get advice from a tax professional in your home country *before* you move.

**Canadians:** Canada taxes based on residency, not citizenship. If you sever residential ties — sell your home, close bank accounts, etc. — you can stop being a Canadian tax resident. If you maintain ties (a Canadian home, a Canadian spouse who stays), you may remain taxable.

### CRS and Financial Transparency

Panama participates in the OECD's Common Reporting Standard (CRS). Panamanian banks share account information with the tax authorities of account holders' home countries. The era of hiding money in Panama is over. What remains is the legal benefit of territorial taxation, which doesn't require hiding anything.

### FATCA (for Americans)

Panamanian banks that have correspondent relationships with U.S. banks (most major ones) report U.S. person account holders to the IRS under FATCA. If you're American and open a Panamanian bank account, the IRS knows about it. This is not a problem if you report it correctly on your FBAR and Form 8938. It becomes a problem if you don't.

## Getting Set Up Properly

If you're planning to relocate to Panama with an eye toward the tax benefits, here's the practical sequence:

1. **Consult a tax professional in your home country** before you leave. Understand your exit tax obligations, any exit filing requirements, and whether a tax treaty between your country and Panama applies.

2. **Establish residency in Panama.** Panama's territorial tax benefits apply to residents. The most common residency paths are the Friendly Nations Visa, Pensionado Visa, and Digital Nomad Visa. Getting a cedula (Panamanian ID) establishes you as a tax resident.

3. **Consult a Panamanian tax attorney** if you have Panamanian-sourced income (local business, rental income, etc.). You'll need to register with the DGI (Panama's tax authority) and file an annual return.

4. **Structure your income correctly.** If you're consulting or freelancing, the difference between "I'm a foreign employee working remotely" and "I'm a contractor serving Panamanian clients" matters for how Panama will classify your income.

## The Bottom Line

Panama's territorial tax system is real, legal, and genuinely valuable for many expats. It's not a secret — it's published in the tax code and has been a core feature of Panama's economy for decades.

The benefit is most clear-cut if your income comes entirely from outside Panama: foreign pensions, remote work for non-Panamanian companies, dividends from foreign portfolios. In those cases, you live in a stable, well-connected country with excellent infrastructure and owe no income tax to Panama.

It's not a blanket "no taxes ever." You still pay VAT on what you buy. You still have obligations in your home country if your home country has a worldwide taxation system. And any income you earn *in* Panama is taxed here.

But for the right profile — a retiree, a remote worker, an investor with foreign assets — it's a meaningful financial advantage that's completely above board.
