In Boquete last October, it rained every single afternoon for eleven days straight — thunder around 2pm, sheets of water by 3, clear skies again by dinner. None of the longtime residents mentioned it. A newly arrived couple two doors down, three weeks into their move, had already started asking around about flights home. Nobody told them Panama has two real seasons, and that one of them is the default state for roughly seven months of the year.

That gap — between what people imagine ("tropical, sunny, warm") and what actually happens for more than half the calendar — is one of the most common sources of regret in the first year here. It's fixable with information, not luck.

## The Two Seasons, By the Calendar

Panama runs on two seasons, not four, and they're driven by the Intertropical Convergence Zone, not by anything resembling a North American or European year.

- **Dry season (verano):** mid-December to mid-April
- **Rainy season (invierno):** mid-April to early December

Yes, "invierno" means winter, and yes, it still hits 85°F (29°C) in the middle of it. Temperature barely moves between seasons — what changes is humidity, cloud cover, and rainfall.

## What Changes Day to Day

### Dry season (December–April)

Skies are mostly clear, humidity drops noticeably, and in Panama City and the Pacific lowlands you can go six to eight weeks without meaningful rain. This is high season for tourism and for property viewings — it's also when most people visiting to "check out Panama before moving" schedule their trip, which skews their impression of what living here feels like year-round.

### Rainy season (May–November)

Expect a predictable pattern more than constant rain: clear mornings, building clouds by early afternoon, a storm lasting 45 minutes to two hours, then clearing again. It is not a monsoon that ruins the day — most mornings are entirely usable for errands, work, or the beach. October and November are the wettest stretch, with multi-day downpours more common, especially in the highlands.

## Regional Differences That Actually Matter

Panama is small, but its microclimates are not. Where you live changes this entire conversation more than the season does.

| Region | Dry season feel | Rainy season feel | Annual rainfall (approx.) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Panama City | Hot, dusty, little rain | Daily afternoon storms, high humidity | 70-80 in / 1,800-2,000 mm |
| Boquete / Chiriquí highlands | Cool mornings, breezy, "bajareque" mist | Heavy, prolonged rain; landslide risk on some roads | 120-140 in / 3,000-3,600 mm |
| Bocas del Toro (Caribbean) | Rain year-round, no true dry season | Rain year-round, slightly heavier | 130+ in / 3,300+ mm |
| Azuero Peninsula (Pedasí) | Very dry, almost desert-like, brush fires possible | Moderate rain, greens up fast | 45-55 in / 1,150-1,400 mm |
| Coronado / Pacific coast | Hot, sunny, beach-perfect | Shorter storms, strong surf | 65-75 in / 1,650-1,900 mm |

Bocas del Toro is the outlier worth flagging: its Caribbean-side weather pattern doesn't follow the Pacific-side calendar, so "rainy season" there is more a matter of degree than a hard switch.

## What Rain Actually Costs You

This is the part most relocation guides skip, and it shows up on your monthly budget whether you planned for it or not.

- **Dehumidifiers and mold prevention:** A decent dehumidifier runs $150-$300, and in Boquete or Bocas you'll likely run two units nearly year-round. Closets without airflow grow mold on leather and books within weeks during peak rainy months.
- **Electricity shifts:** Dry season pushes AC use up in the lowlands — expect $80-$150/month for a small Panama City apartment running AC daily, versus $40-$70/month in rainy season when it's used less.
- **Vehicle and road costs:** Highland dirt roads wash out. If you're in Boquete, Volcán, or similar areas, a 4x4 isn't optional for several months — factor that into your [driving and vehicle planning](/en/driving-in-panama-expat-guide) before you commit to a property up a hillside.
- **Furniture and leather:** Humidity swells wood and rots leather faster than most people expect. Budget for sealed storage or accept shorter furniture lifespans.
- **Trip cancellations:** Inter-island boat transport to Bocas or the San Blas islands gets cancelled more often May through November. Build flexibility into any travel plans during these months.

## What Catches Newcomers Off Guard

The complaint isn't usually the rain itself — it's the mismatch between expectation and reality. People who visited only in February (peak dry season) buy a property, move down in June, and are shocked that "Panama" now means grey skies by 1pm most days. The fix is simple: if you're house-hunting, visit during both seasons, or at minimum visit once in September or October before signing anything long-term.

Power outages also tick up during heavy storms in rural and highland areas — not dramatically, but enough that a backup battery or generator is a reasonable $200-$600 investment if you're off the main grid corridors.

On the upside, rainy season is when Panama is at its most lush — rivers run full, waterfalls are dramatic instead of trickles, and the Chiriquí highlands turn a shade of green that dry season simply doesn't produce. Farmers and gardeners generally prefer it. It's also noticeably quieter: fewer tourists, lower rental demand, and better negotiating leverage if you're looking to rent or buy during these months.

## Planning Your Own Calendar Around It

If you have flexibility, a reasonable approach is to visit during dry season to get the easy win — see the beaches, the views, the lifestyle at its most photogenic — and then come back for at least two weeks between September and November before making a final decision. That second trip tells you more about your actual tolerance for the place than ten dry-season trips combined.

For retirees evaluating the [Pensionado visa](/en/visas/pensionado) and weighing where to settle, this seasonal split is also a strong argument for choosing a region, not just a country. Someone who thrives in Boquete's cool, misty rainy season might find Coronado's brutal dry-season heat unbearable, and vice versa — "Panama weather" isn't one thing.
